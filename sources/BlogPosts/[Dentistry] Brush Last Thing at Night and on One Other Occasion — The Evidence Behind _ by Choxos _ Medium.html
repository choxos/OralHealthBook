[Dentistry] Brush Last Thing at Night and on One Other Occasion — The Evidence Behind

Choxos
7 min read
·
Oct 21, 2023

In a previous piece, I introduced the evidence base for the strongest oral health recommendations by Public Health England and Healthcare Improvement Scotland. In this earlier post, I scrutinized the first recommendation, “Brush twice daily,” and found that the evidence was irrelevant. When assessing a relevant one, the certainty of evidence was low, undermining the proposed “Strong” level of recommendation. Here, I want to assess the second “Strong” recommendation: “Brush last thing at night and on one other occasion.”

This recommendation is derived from a systematic review conducted in 2003, based on an abstract presented at the 48th European Organisation for Caries Research (ORCA) Congress and published in the Caries Research journal (Abstract #63, page 285). The abstract describes a cross-over trial involving 12 participants. In this trial, researchers compared the overnight salivary fluoride clearance of two toothpastes: 1) a chalk-based toothpaste and 2) a silica-based toothpaste. Both toothpastes had the same sodium monofluorophosphate content (1,500 ppmF). The results indicated that fluoride concentrations in saliva 12 hours after brushing at night were similar to those observed 1–4 hours after daytime brushing. As a result, the authors concluded:

Taken together with the findings from various in vitro de- and remineralisation studies, this work indicates that overnight saliva F concentrations from SMFP pastes have the potential to aid beneficially mineralisation processes associated with caries.

Problems

As you may have noticed, the purpose and outcomes of this cross-over trial are not directly related to our primary question: Whether it is advisable to brush our teeth last thing at night and on another occasion. Let’s examine the issues with this research.

Comparison groups

We have some evidence, albeit with low certainty, suggesting that it’s beneficial to brush twice daily. Now, our focus shifts to determining the optimal time of day for brushing our teeth. An ideal study design should involve multiple groups, each following different brushing times. For instance, one group could brush last thing at night and before breakfast, while another group could brush two hours before bedtime and before breakfast. By comparing the outcomes of these two groups, we can determine whether brushing last thing at night is essential. Additionally, we can consider other groups to assess alternative times such as after breakfast or before lunch, depending on the research’s specific objectives and logistical considerations.

However, in the aforementioned trial, both groups brushed “immediately before bedtime,” and the only difference between the two groups was their choice of toothpaste. To address our question, everything should be identical between the two groups, including toothpaste and toothbrush, with the only variable being the timing of toothbrushing. Consequently, the trial’s design is inadequate for addressing our specific question..

Surrogate outcome

I don’t know about you, but if somebody tells me, “Fluoride concentration in your mouth at 12.11 hours after brushing is 0.046 ppmF,” my reaction would be like this:

While outcomes like blood pressure may be important, they aren’t easily perceivable by patients or clinicians. We all fear hypertension because it raises the risk of death. Such outcomes are often referred to as “surrogate outcomes.” In dentistry, many reported outcomes are surrogates, to some degree. In the end, people brush their teeth to prevent tooth pain and tooth loss, not merely to keep their DMFT (Decayed, Missing, Filled Teeth) as low as possible.

Accordingly, dental research should prioritize patient- and clinician-important outcomes, such as pain, function, and quality of life. In the case of that cross-over trial, the outcome (fluoride concentration in saliva) is too abstract to form the basis for such a crucial recommendation. If brushing last thing at night indeed reduces dental pain on average, then everyone would undoubtedly support this recommendation.

Sample size

If someone tells you they’ve made an important scientific discovery based on just 24 participants, would you readily believe them? Personally, I have reservations. With such a small sample size, random fluctuations can significantly influence the results. That’s why studies often involve a larger number of participants to reduce errors in drawing conclusions from the study’s findings.

This process of determining the required number of participants is known as “sample size calculation.” It’s basically hypothesis testing procedure from backwards. In hypothesis testing, you assess whether your data supports your initial hypothesis (the “null hypothesis”). In sample size calculation, you ask, “To detect a specific difference between two groups, if it truly exists, how many participants are needed at a minimum?” This required number depends on the size of the difference. Larger differences require fewer participants. For instance, to test whether water effectively rehydrates individuals, you don’t need a large sample (or even a study). However, most other interventions don’t produce such dramatic effects. Therefore, we need studies with adequate statistical “power” to detect differences.

The issue of underpowered trials and their contribution to research waste has been a subject of ongoing debate and discussion (referenced here and here).

Reporting

It is of paramount importance to thoroughly evaluate the complete study design and results of any research. In this instance, we have access only to an abstract, and therefore, we cannot be certain of the precise details of the trial or whether any additional results exist (selective reporting can be an issue in abstracts). At the very least, we can attempt to contact the authors via email to request further information. It appears that this step may not have been taken in this particular case.

Bias

Both authors are affiliated with “Unilever Dental Research, Bebington, UK.” Unilever is a conglomerate that produces a wide range of products, including oral hygiene items. It’s essential to recognize that while it’s not advisable to outright dismiss research funded by industrial sources, we must remain aware that companies operate for profit. It’s rational for them to be selective in what they publish, favoring studies with more remarkable outcomes. Therefore, when reviewing commercially-sponsored research, it’s prudent to exercise caution. You can explore more on topics like “Industry Sponsorship Bias” and the “Commercial Determinants of Health” for a deeper understanding of these considerations.

Is there any other evidence?

I conducted a search on PubMed to identify articles that have examined various toothbrushing timings. The only somewhat relevant trial I found was a cluster randomized trial (links: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7), with Unilever as the sponsor once again. In this trial, they investigated a program named “Brush Day & Night” (formerly “Live.Learn.Laugh”), which was jointly developed by the FDI World Dental Federation and Unilever.

In one of the phases of the trial, over 4,000 children in Indonesia and Nigeria were divided into two groups: 1) the 21-day Brush Day & Night program, and 2) a group that did not receive the 21-day program but only toothbrushes and toothpaste. After 6 months, both groups reported experiencing less mouth pain, with minimal differences observed between the two groups. Notably, even in Indonesia, more children in the control group reported having “no mouth pain” compared to those in the intervention group (see Figure 2 in this link or the figure below).

Caveats

This research highlights the effectiveness of an instructional program aimed at improving the oral health of children. While it may not directly address our specific question, it is a valuable study, especially considering the challenges of designing a trial to closely monitor all participants.

Nevertheless, for our question, a more directly relevant comparison group would be individuals not brushing their teeth last thing at night. However, this research suggests that it might be more effective to provide oral hygiene instruments initially and then focus on education. People tend to make better choices when they have options and the knowledge to make informed decisions.

Take home notes

Once again, it appears that another strong oral health recommendation lacks relevant supporting evidence. The limited semi-relevant evidence available doesn’t endorse the practice of brushing last thing at night. While I personally believe it’s a beneficial practice and have followed it for many years, the need for high-quality evidence to encourage others to adopt this habit remains. We’ll eagerly await the day when such robust evidence becomes available.
